How the score works, and its limits
  • Confirmed: if Chess.com has closed the account for fair-play violations, risk is shown as 99%. That's the only hard evidence.
  • Accuracy vs. expected: uses the game-review accuracy Chess.com stores on a game (it only exists for reviewed games). Expected accuracy is a rough curve from their rating (about 64% at 800, 76% at 1600, 88% at 2400). One great game means little; a pattern does.
  • New account (under 30 / 90 / 365 days), rating jump since your game (+150 / +300), closed account for other reasons, and a 70%+ win rate over 50+ games each add weight.
  • Weights go through a logistic curve, starting near 4% with no signals. They're hand-set, not trained on Chess.com's data, so read the score as a ranking for what to review, not a probability you can prove.
  • Engine check: replays the games in Stockfish 10 in your browser. From move 9 on, it skips forced moves and positions already decided (over 5 pawns), then measures how often each side played the engine's top move or a top-3 move, and the average centipawn loss (how much worse each move was than the best, in hundredths of a pawn). Your own moves in the same games are the baseline. It also compares how evenly each side spent time per move (from the clock data in the game record): suspiciously even timing is a known red flag. "Typical" ranges are rough figures for blitz and rapid and shift with engine depth. A strong player can match the engine often without cheating, and a careful cheater may only use it a few times per game.
  • Report: shown for active players with risk of 25% or more, or an engine check showing signs. It copies an evidence note (game links, engine figures, other signals) and opens their profile. There, click ⋯ → Report, choose Cheating, and paste the note.
  • Chess.com's own detection uses move-level data it doesn't publish. Report suspicions to Chess.com rather than accusing a player in public.
